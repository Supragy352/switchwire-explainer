Voron Switchwire · bed slinger mechanics

Switchwire, part by part

The print bed is the heaviest thing on this printer, and it is also the thing that has to accelerate hardest. Almost every design decision on the Switchwire follows from that.

Built from the Switchwire CAD assembly (Voron repository, January 2025). The model alongside is the real assembly, 781 parts. It follows the section you are reading. Numbers marked ≈ are order-of-magnitude estimates, not measurements of a particular machine.

1 What a Switchwire is

The Switchwire is a bed slinger: the print bed moves back and forth to make the Y axis, while the toolhead moves left–right (X) and up–down (Z) on a gantry driven by a CoreXZ belt system. Every part on its bill of materials does one of five jobs: hold geometry fixed (frame), constrain motion to one degree of freedom (rails), transmit force (belts, pulleys, motors), put heat in the right place and nowhere else (bed and hotend), or sense and decide (electronics and firmware).

Each block below is clickable and highlights that subsystem in the model.

Why a bed slinger is worth studying

Most hobby printers are either bed slingers (Prusa i3, Ender 3) or CoreXY machines (Voron 2.4, Trident, Bambu X1). The Switchwire sits between them. It keeps the bed slinger's simple open frame but borrows the CoreXY idea of two stationary motors working together through a crossed belt path, applied to X and Z instead of X and Y.

A heated aluminium bed with a steel sheet weighs on the order of a kilogram, several times a typical toolhead. Since F = ma, and the frame must react every newton the motor pushes into the bed, nearly every choice on the Y axis is about managing that inertia. Section 9 works it through.

Coordinate conventions

  • X: left–right, along the gantry.
  • Y: front–back, the direction the bed travels. Moving the bed backward is the same as moving the nozzle forward relative to the part.
  • Z: vertical. Z is a gantry axis, not a moving bed: the whole X gantry rises as the print grows.
  • A and B: the two CoreXZ motors. In the CAD they are called "X motor" (A, left) and "Z motor" (B, right), but neither alone moves X or Z. Their sum and difference do.

2 Kinematics

The Switchwire combines a plain Cartesian Y axis (one motor, one belt, the bed) with a CoreXZ system for X and Z (two motors, two belts, both coupled to the toolhead). Klipper calls the combination corexz.

2.1A single belt axis

A stepper turns a toothed pulley; the pulley pulls a toothed belt clamped to the moving object. With a pulley of N teeth and belt pitch p, one revolution moves the belt by

\Delta s_{\text{rev}} = N p

For the 20-tooth GT2 pulley (p = 2 mm) that is 40 mm, Klipper's rotation_distance. A 1.8° stepper has 200 full steps per revolution; with m microsteps the commanded increment is

\delta s = \frac{N p}{200\, m} = \frac{40\ \text{mm}}{3200} = 12.5\ \mu\text{m} \quad (m = 16)

That is the resolution of commands, not of position: belt stretch, pulley eccentricity and microstep torque (Section 5) limit real accuracy. The official SKR configuration uses 32 microsteps, halving the increment to 6.25 µm. The Y axis is exactly this arrangement: motor at the rear, belt loop under the bed, idler at the front, belt ends clamped to the bed carriage.

2.2The CoreXZ idea

Both motors are fixed to the frame. Each belt climbs from its motor to a fixed idler at the top, crosses the machine, drops to an idler on the moving gantry and ends at the X carriage. It is CoreXY rotated 90° to stand vertically. With belt displacements a and b from motors A and B:

\Delta X = \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta a + \Delta b), \qquad \Delta Z = \tfrac{1}{2}(\Delta a - \Delta b)

and the firmware inverts it:

\Delta a = \Delta X + \Delta Z, \qquad \Delta b = \Delta X - \Delta Z

Signs depend on routing and motor direction; Klipper absorbs them in dir_pin. The consequences matter more:

  1. A pure X move turns both motors the same way. Each needs roughly half the torque a single X motor would.
  2. A pure Z move turns them in opposite directions. One side's belt shortens and the other's lengthens, lifting the gantry.
  3. One motor alone moves the nozzle diagonally, 45° in the XZ plane, by half its belt travel in each axis. If one driver fails or skips, the nozzle drifts diagonally, a useful diagnostic.
  4. The work is shared. Every move can use the power of both motors, unlike a Cartesian machine where an X move uses only the X motor.

2.3Gravity in a belt-driven Z

A leadscrew Z axis is self-locking; a belt-driven one is not. In CoreXZ the gantry weight W hangs on the belts, and each motor's pulley must resist

\tau_{\text{hold}} \approx \frac{W}{2} \cdot r_{\text{pulley}}

For ≈1 kg of gantry and toolhead on a 20T pulley (pitch radius ≈6.4 mm) that is ≈0.03 N·m per motor, a small fraction of a NEMA 17's holding torque. The motors hold easily while energised, but with drivers off the gantry slides down, and gravity biases every Z move. The Switchwire fixes this mechanically: a Key-Bak Super 48 spring retractor, the kind used for keys on a belt, is tied to the gantry over an idler of two F695 bearings and pulls upward with a nearly constant force close to the gantry's weight. The net belt load is then small whether the motors are on or off. Klipper doesn't model the residual imbalance; the motors simply have margin.

2.4Why not CoreXY, or a moving bed in Z?

A CoreXY machine keeps the heavy bed out of the fast axes and moves it slowly in Z, but needs an enclosed cube frame and either a bed on leadscrews or a gantry on four Z motors. The Switchwire lets the bed move fast in Y and takes it out of Z: a simpler, open frame with three motion motors plus the extruder, at the cost of a heavy Y axis.

2.5Equations of motion for one axis

Model any axis as a mass driven through a belt of stiffness k by a motor whose rotor has inertia J on a pulley of radius r. The rotor adds an apparent linear mass J/r^2:

m_{\text{eff}} = m_{\text{load}} + \frac{J_{\text{rotor}}}{r^2}

A typical NEMA 17 rotor has J \approx 5 \times 10^{-6} kg·m², so with r = 6.4 mm it adds ≈0.1 kg: small next to the bed, not small next to a light toolhead. The belt is a spring between pulley and load, so a step change in pulley position excites oscillation at

f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}

That one equation explains ringing, why stiffer belts and lighter carriages help, and why input shaping works (Section 9).

3 Frame, structure and linear motion

The frame holds every rail, motor and idler in fixed relation while the motors push against it. Every compliance in the frame sits in series with the belts and lowers the machine's resonant frequency, so the frame is part of the motion system, not just a stand.

An extrusion is aluminium alloy (typically 6063-T5 or 6061) pushed through a die to a constant T-slotted cross-section. Its shape puts material far from the neutral axis, which is exactly what bending stiffness rewards.

3.2Bending stiffness, and why the base is 3060

Bending resistance is EI: Young's modulus times second moment of area. For a solid rectangle I = bh^3/12, so stiffness grows with the cube of depth; a 3060 on its 60 mm face is six to eight times stiffer vertically than a 3030. For a cantilever of length L with tip load F:

\delta = \frac{F L^3}{3 E I}

The L^3 term is why the uprights are short, stout 3030 tied together at the top. A 400 mm free cantilever deflects almost three times as much as a 280 mm one; bracing the top makes it a beam fixed at both ends, up to sixteen times stiffer for a central load.

The X beam is a 2020 spanning ≈340 mm between the Z carriages. Simply supported with the toolhead at mid-span, k = 48EI/L^3; with E \approx 69 GPa and I \approx 7 \times 10^3 mm⁴, k \approx 6 \times 10^5 N/m and a 0.5 kg toolhead resonates near 170 Hz, well above the Y axis. The weaker direction is torsion: the toolhead hangs in front of the beam, and a 2020's torsional stiffness is far below its bending stiffness.

3.3Joints, fasteners and preload

The frame uses blind joints: an M8×16 button-head screw passes through an access hole in one extrusion into the tapped end of the next, eight in total. The joint is clamped, not pinned; its stiffness is friction between faces under bolt tension:

T \approx K F d

With nut factor K \approx 0.2, an M8 at 10 N·m gives F \approx 6 kN of clamp. While the faces never slip the joint is nearly rigid; once they slip, the frame can rack out of square. Elsewhere the BOM is dozens of M3 and M5 screws, T-nuts, and ≈70 brass heat-set inserts: threads cut straight into ABS strip and creep, while a knurled insert spreads the load and gives a durable metal thread.

3.4Panels, feet and damping

A rectangular frame resists bending well but resists shear (racking into a parallelogram) only through its joints. A panel fastened along its edges acts as a shear diaphragm, the same reason a box is stiffer than an open frame. The CAD panels are 3–4 mm; material is the builder's choice.

The four printed TPU feet decouple the printer from the table. TPU is a soft, lossy elastomer: it forms a low-frequency spring that lets the frame rock on fast Y moves (Section 9.7) but absorbs high-frequency motor noise. Builders trade the two by changing foot hardness or standing the printer on a heavy slab.

3.5Linear guides: rails and carriages

A linear guide removes five of a carriage's six degrees of freedom while letting the sixth move with almost no friction. The Switchwire uses miniature MGN profile rails rather than the V-slot wheels or smooth rods of budget bed slingers.

3.6How a profile rail works

The rail is hardened steel with precision-ground raceways. Inside the carriage, two rows of steel balls recirculate: they roll between carriage and rail in the loaded zone, then return through a channel in the body and plastic end caps. Rolling instead of sliding gives friction coefficients of 0.002–0.005, two orders of magnitude below a plain bushing.

The raceways meet each ball at an angle (a gothic-arch profile, typically 45°), so each row resists load in two directions; two rows resist vertical load, lateral load and moments about all three axes. That is why one carriage per rail is enough: four 300 mm MGN12H rails (two Z, one X, one Y), each with a single long H carriage. The bed rides on just one block.

3.7Rail sizes

MGN rails are named by width: MGN9 is 9 mm, MGN12 is 12 mm. Larger rails carry larger balls and much higher moment stiffness. The H suffix marks a long carriage. For a moment M resisted by two contact zones a distance d apart, each sees ≈M/d, so doubling d halves the load and the deflection.

3.8Preload

Preload is built-in interference between balls and raceways, from balls slightly larger than the gap. Hertzian contact is nonlinear, so stiffness rises with load:

\delta \propto F^{2/3}, \qquad k = \frac{dF}{d\delta} \propto F^{1/3}

A preloaded carriage starts partway up this curve: no play at zero load, higher stiffness for small loads, at the cost of friction and wear. Printer rails are usually Z0 or ZA (zero or light). Too much preload feels notchy and wastes torque; too little lets the carriage rock, which prints as layer-to-layer wobble.

3.9Moment loads on a bed slinger

The bed's centre of mass sits a height h above the rail, so accelerating it creates a pitching moment:

M_{\text{pitch}} = m_{\text{bed}}\, a\, h

With 1.4 kg, 5000 mm/s² and h = 30 mm, M \approx 0.21 N·m: small, but repeated millions of times. Any rocking in the carriage turns into vertical motion at the bed edges, which looks like Z wobble. One long H carriage keeps it small.

3.10Mounting and alignment

A rail is only as straight as what it is bolted to: extrusion is straight to roughly 0.1 mm per metre and the rail follows its bow. Parallel rails must be parallel within the carriage's tolerance or they fight each other. Builders align with a straightedge or printed guides and tighten from the centre out. Rails ship in preservative oil, not lubricant: clean them, then grease or oil them per the Voron docs. Dry balls wear the raceways gritty within a few hundred hours.

3.11Rail failure modes

  • Lost balls: pulling a carriage off without a retaining strip spills balls; it then binds or rattles.
  • Brinelling: a sharp impact dents the raceway, leaving a permanent notch.
  • Corrosion: rails are often carbon steel and rust if left dry in a humid room.
  • Racking: misaligned parallel rails bind at one end of travel and run free at the other.

4 Belts, pulleys and idlers

Belts are the springs of the motion system: they carry every newton from motor to load, and their stiffness and tooth engagement set both positional accuracy and resonant frequency. The Switchwire uses 6 mm GT2 belt (2 mm pitch) throughout. In the model the three belts are drawn from the real pulley positions, so they follow the axes when you move them.

4.1Anatomy of a GT2 belt

Three layers: a moulded elastomer body (neoprene or polyurethane) carrying the teeth, tensile cords (fibreglass in good belts, sometimes steel or aramid) along its length, and often a nylon facing on the teeth. The cords carry almost all the load and set the stiffness. GT2's rounded tooth enters and leaves the pulley with less sliding and backlash than trapezoidal MXL or T2.5, which matters at every direction reversal, every corner of a print.

4.2Stiffness and stretch

Under tension T, a span of length L stretches by

\Delta L = \frac{T L}{EA}

where EA is the belt's axial stiffness, dominated by the cords. A 9 mm belt is about 1.5× stiffer than a 6 mm one; fibreglass creeps little; steel cord is stiffer but fatigues on small pulleys; cheap polyester-cord belts stretch and creep over months. Dynamically the belt is the spring in f_n; statically, when the motor accelerates the load the belt first stretches by F/k, so the nozzle lags its commanded position by up to a few tens of microns.

4.3Tension

Pre-tension keeps the slack span from going slack during acceleration, so both spans act as springs in parallel, and slightly raises the effective stiffness. Too little allows tooth skipping and backlash; too much loads motor and idler bearings and speeds wear. The practical check is to pluck a span and measure its note, since a string of length L, tension T and linear density \mu vibrates at

f = \frac{1}{2L}\sqrt{\frac{T}{\mu}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = \mu\,(2 L f)^2

4.4Drive pulleys

The drive pulley is fixed to the motor shaft with grub screws against the shaft's flat. Its tooth count sets the gearing: fewer teeth give more force per unit torque (F = \tau/r) and finer resolution, but fewer teeth engaged and a tighter bend. With 20 teeth and ≈180° of wrap, about 10 teeth engage. Pulley eccentricity is a hidden error source: a bore off-centre by e makes the effective radius vary once per revolution, printing faint banding every 40 mm for a 20T pulley.

4.5Idlers

Idlers redirect the belt, either toothed (teeth facing the belt teeth) or smooth (running on the flat back). Most are flanged ball bearings, F695 and F623 being common, stacked or pressed into a toothed shell. The bearing carries twice the tension times the sine of half the wrap angle:

F_{\text{bearing}} = 2T\sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right)

At 180° that is 2T: about 18 N at ≈9 N of tension, the figure Voron's tuning guide gives for its A/B belts (110 Hz over a 150 mm span, about 2 lb, at the low end of the range), well within a small bearing's rating but enough to show as wear on a poor bearing or a flexing shaft. Flanges keep the belt from walking off; a belt that always rubs one flange points to a misaligned pulley or idler.

4.6Belt clamps

Belt ends are clamped to the moving carriage by folding the belt over itself so the teeth interlock, then trapping it in a printed slot. The load passes in shear across many teeth instead of relying on friction. A loose clamp is a soft spring in series with the belt and lowers resonance far more than the belt itself.

5 Motors: NEMA 17 steppers

The three motion axes use 1.8° NEMA 17 hybrid steppers; the extruder uses a small round NEMA 14 pancake (the CAD models an LDO-36STH20). "NEMA 17" fixes only the faceplate, 1.7 in (≈42.3 mm) square with a standard bolt pattern. Torque, current, inductance and step angle vary by model, and they decide how fast the machine can move.

5.1How a hybrid stepper works

A permanent-magnet rotor sits between two toothed iron cups offset by half a tooth, so it presents alternating north and south teeth, typically 50. The stator has eight toothed poles carrying two phase windings, A and B. Energising a phase pulls the rotor to the position of least magnetic reluctance. With 50 teeth and a four-state sequence:

\theta_{\text{step}} = \frac{360^\circ}{4 \times 50} = 1.8^\circ

A 0.9° motor has 100 teeth and 400 steps per revolution: finer and often smoother, but it needs twice the step rate for the same shaft speed.

5.2Torque versus angle, and microstepping

With phase currents I_A and I_B, the torque at electrical angle \theta_e is approximately

\tau(\theta_e) \approx k_t \left( -I_A \sin\theta_e + I_B \cos\theta_e \right)

The rotor rests where torque is zero. Driving I_A = I\cos\varphi, I_B = I\sin\varphi moves that rest point smoothly to \theta_e = \varphi: microstepping. Two cautions follow. The restoring torque for a small error \delta is k_t I \sin\delta, so the torque that holds one microstep against a load shrinks with microstep size; microsteps buy smoothness, not precise positioning under load. And real motors deviate from sinusoidal torque, so microsteps are unequal, printing a fine periodic pattern called VFA (vertical fine artifacts).

5.3The electrical side: inductance and back-EMF

Each phase is an inductor L with resistance R, and the driver chops a supply voltage across it to regulate current:

V = I R + L \frac{dI}{dt} + k_e\,\omega

The last term is back-EMF, proportional to shaft speed. At high speed, back-EMF and the L\,dI/dt needed to reverse current every step consume the supply voltage, the current waveform collapses and torque falls: the torque–speed curve is flat at low speed and drops past a corner speed. Higher supply voltage and lower inductance move the corner up, which is why Vorons favour 24 V and some builders run motion drivers at 48 V.

5.4Holding torque, current and heat

A typical 1.8° NEMA 17 of 40–48 mm body length holds ≈0.4–0.55 N·m at 1.5–2 A. Klipper sets RMS current in software (run_current); running below rating cuts torque roughly in proportion and heat with the square (P = I^2R per phase). Motors run comfortably at 60–80 °C, but PLA mounts soften above ≈55 °C, one reason Voron parts are printed in ABS or ASA.

5.5Mid-band resonance

A stepper rotor is a mass on a magnetic spring; at some speeds the step frequency couples with its natural oscillation and the motor loses torque or stalls. TMC drivers damp this differently in StealthChop (voltage mode: quiet, but loses torque and can skip at speed) and SpreadCycle (current mode: louder, more robust). Many Voron configs use SpreadCycle on the motion axes.

6 The moving bed

The bed is a Prusa MK52-style heated bed: a 24 V DC heater etched as a copper trace on an aluminium-core PCB, ≈250 × 210 mm, with embedded magnets that hold a removable spring-steel sheet. It is both a heater and the Y axis payload, so its design balances thermal performance against moving mass.

Unlike many bed slingers there is no separate machined plate and no levelling springs: the PCB is the plate, screwed down on rigid spacers. Levelling is done in software with the inductive probe and Klipper's bed mesh (Section 8). The bed wiring needs ≥1 mm² (AWG18) heater wire with ring terminals.

6.2Joule heating

P = \frac{V^2}{R}

The MK52 is commonly quoted near 220 W at 24 V (unverified for clones), implying R \approx 2.6\ \Omega and ≈9 A. That current is why the manual asks for at least 1 mm² wire to the bed and 2.5 mm² from the supply to the board: wire resistance turns current into heat, and a thin, flexing bed wire is a classic fire point. The board switches the heater with a MOSFET on its HB output, under Klipper's PID control.

6.3Heat-up time and steady state

The bed's heat capacity is roughly the sum of mc over its parts:

C \approx m_{\text{Al}} c_{\text{Al}} + m_{\text{steel}} c_{\text{steel}} \approx 0.55(900) + 0.25(470) \approx 610\ \text{J/K}

Heating 25 → 100 °C needs Q = C\Delta T \approx 46 kJ, ≈3.5 minutes at 220 W with no losses. Losses stretch it, especially near the target: convection from both faces (≈0.105 m², h \approx 10 W/m²K) is ≈80 W at 100 °C, and the PEI top radiates

P_{\text{rad}} = \varepsilon \sigma A (T^4 - T_0^4) \approx 0.9 \times 5.67\times10^{-8} \times 0.0525 \times (373^4 - 298^4) \approx 31\ \text{W}

So at 100 °C the bed needs ≈110 W just to stay hot, half its capacity. That is why ABS and ASA (bed at 100–110 °C) benefit from an enclosure: warm air cuts losses. The configuration caps the bed at 130 °C.

6.4The thermistor and the control loop

An NTC thermistor's resistance falls steeply with temperature. The simple beta model:

R(T) = R_0 \exp\!\left[\beta\left(\frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_0}\right)\right]

with R_0 = 100 kΩ at 25 °C and \beta \approx 3950 K. The board reads it through a voltage divider; sensitivity is highest near room temperature and falls as it heats, which is why NTCs become inaccurate above ≈280–300 °C. Klipper runs PID (heater duty = K_p e + K_i \int e\,dt + K_d\, de/dt), and PID_CALIBRATE fits the gains to the bed's thermal lag. Thermal runaway protection shuts down if the temperature fails to rise or drifts from target, which only works if the thermistor stays bonded to the bed: one that falls off reads low and the controller drives the heater flat out. The manual lists no separate thermal fuse; many builders add one as a hardware backstop.

6.5Magnets and the spring-steel sheet

Neodymium magnets lose remanence at about 0.1% per kelvin, reversibly below their grade's maximum working temperature; beyond it the loss is permanent. Standard N grades are rated to ≈80 °C; H and SH grades tolerate more. A bed that sheds its sheet's grip after months of hot printing often has over-heated magnets. Spring steel is chosen because it is ferromagnetic and has a high yield strength, so it flexes to release parts and springs back flat. PEI, smooth or textured, bonds to hot PLA, PETG and ABS and releases as it cools.

6.6Thermal expansion and bed flatness

Aluminium expands at \alpha \approx 23 \times 10^{-6}/K. Over 250 mm and a 75 K rise:

\Delta L = \alpha L \Delta T = 23\times10^{-6} \times 250\ \text{mm} \times 75 \approx 0.43\ \text{mm}

The steel sheet grows about half as much. A bed hotter on one face bows like a bimetallic strip, doming by tens of microns, which matters under a 0.2 mm first layer. The remedy is procedural: heat-soak, then probe the mesh at printing temperature. Screws fixing the bed must let it expand; a bed clamped rigidly at four corners has nowhere to go and bows.

6.7The bed as a moving mass

Everything above rides on the Y carriage. Every extra gram of plate or magnet lowers the Y resonance and raises the force per unit acceleration (Section 9). The thin PCB bed is a deliberate choice for low mass at some cost in thermal uniformity compared with a thick cast plate.

7 Toolhead and extruder

The current release uses the Voron StealthBurner toolhead with a Clockwork 2 extruder (older revisions used the Afterburner). Its job is to push filament at a controlled rate, melt it in a small, well-defined zone and freeze it again the instant it leaves the nozzle, while staying as light as possible because it rides the CoreXZ gantry.

7.2The extruder: gears and grip

The Clockwork 2 squeezes filament between two hobbed gears. The motor's 10-tooth pinion drives a 50-tooth gear (5:1); that gear turns one drive gear, which meshes with the second. Gripping from both sides balances the lateral force and doubles the teeth biting in. The filament force available is

F_{\text{fil}} = \frac{\tau_{\text{motor}} \times 5}{r_{\text{drive}}}

Klipper's rotation_distance = 22.68 mm with that ratio gives an effective drive diameter of 22.68/π ≈ 7.2 mm, so r \approx 3.6 mm. A modest 0.1 N·m at the motor puts ≈140 N on the filament, far more than the 10–40 N typical extrusion needs. The real limit is grip: past what the teeth can hold, they chew a groove and extrusion stops. The idler's spring and thumbscrew set the normal force: too little and the gears slip, too much and they crush soft filament.

7.3The hotend: a heat engine in reverse

Four thermal zones, and the design problem is making the cold-to-hot transition as short as possible. Filament that softens above the heat break swells and jams: heat creep.

  1. Drive gearsCold, solid filament; 5:1 reduction
  2. Heat sink + hotend fanRemoves heat that leaks up; filament stays solid
  3. Heat breakThin wall, low conductivity: R = L / kA is large
  4. Heater block (melt zone)Heater cartridge and thermistor; filament melts here
  5. NozzleOrifice sets line width; plastic leaves molten
R_{\text{th}} = \frac{L}{k A}

A thin, low-k wall (stainless or titanium, sometimes copper-bimetal) makes R large so little heat leaks upward, and the finned sink and fan remove what does. That is why Klipper configures the hotend fan as a heater_fan that switches on above 50 °C.

7.4Melting rate and the flow limit

Volumetric flow is speed × line width × layer height: 0.45 × 0.2 mm lines at 150 mm/s are 13.5 mm³/s. Heating that PLA takes little power:

P_{\text{melt}} = Q \rho c \Delta T \approx 13.5\times10^{-9} \times 1240 \times 1800 \times 190 \approx 5.7\ \text{W}

A 40–60 W heater supplies that easily. The real limit is conduction time: heat diffuses into 1.75 mm filament on a timescale t \sim r^2/\alpha, several seconds for the core with \alpha \approx 10^{-7} m²/s, while filament crosses the melt zone in under a second at high flow. Longer melt zones (Volcano, Rapido UHF) and designs that split the stream (core-heating Revo variants, CHT nozzles) attack that diffusion time, not heater power.

7.5Pressure advance

Molten plastic is viscous and the filament path acts like a spring: pressure must build before flow starts, and residual pressure keeps oozing after the extruder stops, giving thin starts and blobby ends. Klipper's pressure advance drives the extruder ahead of the commanded flow:

v_{\text{ext,adj}} = v_{\text{ext}} + K_{\text{PA}} \frac{dv_{\text{ext}}}{dt}

K_{\text{PA}} is tuned per filament and hotend; a short direct-drive path keeps it small.

7.6Part cooling

A thin line loses heat at hA(T - T_{\text{air}}), and forced convection raises h several-fold. Ducting matters as much as fan size: a jet aimed just below the nozzle cools the line without chilling the tip. PLA wants strong cooling; ABS and ASA want little, because rapid cooling raises shrinkage stress and layer separation.

7.7Mass budget

The toolhead's mass sets X acceleration through the CoreXZ belts and adds to the gantry weight the motors hold against gravity, hence short-body motors, compact hotends and thin-walled printed parts. But on the Switchwire, unlike a CoreXY, the toolhead is not the speed limit; the bed is. That leaves room for a heavier, higher-flow hotend without slowing the machine.

The electronics turn mains into regulated 24 V and 5 V, drive four steppers and two heaters, read thermistors and switches, and run Klipper split between a Raspberry Pi (the planner) and a microcontroller board (the real-time executor). The official design targets a BTT SKR Mini E3 (V2 or V3) or, for builders reusing Prusa parts, an Einsy Rambo.

8.3The controller board and stepper drivers

The SKR Mini E3 carries a 32-bit ARM microcontroller and four TMC2209 drivers configured over UART (the Einsy uses TMC2130 over SPI). Each driver is an H-bridge per phase with current sensing, chopping 24 V to hold each phase at its target. The official configs run 0.5 A for X, Y and Z and 0.7 A for the extruder, with 32 microsteps on the SKR (16 on the Einsy): conservative values that keep board and motors cool at the cost of torque, which is why the board has its own 6020 fan. The heater outputs are MOSFETs: at 9 A and 10 mΩ R_{DS(on)}, under 1 W, but a worn connector adds contact resistance in series and can overheat at the same current.

8.4Klipper's split architecture

Klipper runs its motion planner, corexz kinematics, input shaper and pressure advance on the Raspberry Pi in Python, compiles a precise step schedule and sends it to the microcontroller, which only emits steps at the right times and reports sensors. The heavy maths runs on a fast general-purpose processor; the MCU's timing stays deterministic.

  2. LRS-350-24 → SKR Mini E3All motor and heater power: 4 steppers, bed and hotend heat, fans, sensors and probe
  3. RS-25-5 → Raspberry PiKlipper host and web interface, on its own 5 V
  4. Pi ⇄ SKR over USBOnly the step schedule and sensor data cross this link

8.5Endstops: microswitches wired normally closed

X and Y home against microswitches wired through the common and normally-closed contacts: the circuit is closed in normal operation and opens when pressed. A broken wire also opens it, so the firmware sees "triggered" and stops instead of driving the axis into the frame. Repeatability, often a few microns, comes from the snap-action spring blade flipping over centre at a fixed force. Y can instead home sensorlessly using the TMC2209's StallGuard; the config includes it as a commented option.

8.6The inductive probe and Z

Z has no endstop switch. It homes, and the bed is meshed, with an inductive probe on the toolhead (the manual pictures a PL-08N and recommends an Omron TL-Q5MC). The probe drives an LC oscillator; near a conductor its field induces eddy currents that dissipate energy and damp the oscillation, and past a threshold the output switches. The physics explains its quirks:

  • Material dependence. Trigger distance depends on the target's conductivity and permeability; steel triggers farther than aluminium, so changing sheets can change the Z offset.
  • Temperature drift. Coil resistance and oscillator parameters drift as toolhead and bed warm. Probe at operating temperature.
  • Supply voltage. Designed for 6–36 V; power from 24 V, not 5 V, or switching turns erratic.

The probe output can swing to 24 V but the MCU input tolerates 3.3 V. The BOM's BAT85 Schottky diode sits in the signal line: the board's pull-up holds the input high; when the probe's NPN output pulls low, the diode conducts and drags the input low too; when the output goes high, the diode blocks 24 V from the pin. Klipper uses the probe as a virtual Z endstop (probe:z_virtual_endstop) and for BED_MESH_CALIBRATE, then adjusts Z continuously to follow the measured bed.

8.7Cable chains and moving wires

Three 10 × 11 mm cable chains carry wires to X, Y and Z. Copper fails by fatigue under repeated bending: each bend cycles the strands' strain and cracks grow until they break, and fatigue life falls steeply with strain amplitude, which the bend radius sets. Chains enforce a minimum radius and keep flexing in one plane. The manual warns that ordinary component leads are not chain-rated and recommends crimping onto high-flex wire with many fine strands. In the model the chains stay at their CAD pose when you move the axes.

8.8Earthing and safety

Protective earth bonds the PSU cases and the aluminium frame. If a live conductor chafes against the frame, fault current flows to earth and trips the breaker or RCD instead of energising the machine. One solid bond per extrusion assembly, through anodising-piercing hardware or a star washer, is enough. Skipping earthing on a metal-framed printer is relying on luck.

9 Bed-slinger physics

A bed slinger's speed limit is set by the Y axis: a heavy, compliantly driven mass whose resonance sits low enough to print visible ringing unless the motion is shaped. This section builds that claim from first principles; order-of-magnitude numbers are labelled as estimates.

9.1How heavy is the Y axis?

ComponentEstimateBasis

A StealthBurner-class toolhead with Clockwork extruder is ≈0.4–0.6 kg, so the bed is two to three times heavier. Below, m_{\text{eff}} \approx 1.4 kg (an upper-end bed plus the Y rotor's apparent mass) is used as a conservative figure. With the same motors everywhere, for equal force the bed accelerates at a third to half the toolhead's rate.

9.2Force, torque and the acceleration ceiling

Suppose at printing speed a NEMA 17 delivers \tau \approx 0.2 N·m (an estimate; it depends on motor, current and voltage). Through a 20T GT2 pulley:

F = \frac{\tau}{r} = \frac{0.2\ \text{N·m}}{6.37\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}} \approx 31\ \text{N}

With 1.4 kg that allows a \approx 22 m/s², about 22 000 mm/s². Real bed slingers print at a few thousand. The limit is not torque. It is vibration, and the part on the bed.

9.3The belt as a spring, and why stiffness depends on bed position

A belt span of length L has stiffness EA/L (for reinforced GT2, EA is of order 10⁵ N). The carriage is pulled by two spans in parallel, pulley-to-carriage L_1 and carriage-around-the-idler-and-back L_2:

k_Y = EA\left(\frac{1}{L_1} + \frac{1}{L_2}\right), \qquad L_1 + L_2 = L_{\text{loop}}

This is smallest with the bed mid-travel (L_1 = L_2) and rises sharply near either end. For EA = 10^5 N and a 0.6 m loop, k_Y \approx 6.7 \times 10^5 N/m at the centre, and with 1.4 kg:

f_n = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{6.7\times10^{5}}{1.4}} \approx 110\ \text{Hz}

The model still predicts something real: resonance shifts as the bed moves, so a single input-shaper frequency is a compromise.

9.4Ringing

When the commanded velocity changes abruptly at a corner, the belt–mass system rings at f_n, decaying with damping ratio \zeta (typically 0.05–0.15 for printer axes):

x(t) = A\, e^{-\zeta\omega_n t}\sin(\omega_d t), \qquad \omega_d = \omega_n\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}

The nozzle keeps laying plastic, so the oscillation is printed into the wall as ripples spaced v/f_d apart. At 100 mm/s and 40 Hz they sit 2.5 mm apart, exactly the ghosting on test prints. Measuring that spacing with calipers is a cheap way to estimate f_d.

9.5Input shaping

Input shaping cancels the oscillation by design rather than by stiffness. Replace each acceleration step with two smaller steps half a damped period apart; the second step's oscillation is in antiphase with the first and they cancel. The zero-vibration (ZV) shaper's two impulses are

A_1 = \frac{1}{1+K}, \quad A_2 = \frac{K}{1+K}, \quad K = \exp\!\left(\frac{-\zeta\pi}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\right), \quad \Delta t = \frac{\pi}{\omega_d}

Robustness matters on a bed slinger because f_n changes along the Y travel and as the print gets heavier. Klipper measures resonance with an accelerometer (ADXL345 or similar) on the bed for Y and on the toolhead for X, sweeps the excitation frequency, fits each shaper, and reports a maximum recommended acceleration per shaper; above it, corners visibly blur.

9.6The print rides on the bed

A tall, slender part of mass m_p with its centre of mass at height h feels m_p a at that height, bending it at the base:

M_{\text{base}} = m_p\, a\, h

The part is a cantilever whose tip deflects ≈Fh^3/(3EI), so the nozzle meets a moving target; thin towers and tall vases print noticeably worse on bed slingers. The same force acts on first-layer adhesion: if m_p a exceeds what the PEI bond provides, the part slides or pops off mid-print. CoreXY machines avoid both because their bed does not move in X or Y.

9.7Momentum and the frame

Every newton pushed into the bed pushes the frame the other way. With frame mass M, the frame recoils at F/M unless the feet hold it: at 31 N on a 7 kg machine, ≈4.4 m/s², well above what rubber feet on a desk will hold, so fast Y moves rock or walk the printer. That rocking is a low-frequency mode in series with the belt, which is why heavy, wide-footed bed slingers on solid tables print better. Klipper's resonance test shows the difference when you add mass or bolt the printer down.

9.8Energy and power

Kinetic energy at 300 mm/s is only \tfrac{1}{2} \times 1.4 \times 0.3^2 \approx 0.06 J; peak mechanical power Fv \approx 31 \times 0.3 \approx 9 W. Neither is a limit. The thermal budget belongs to the heaters; the motion budget is set by stiffness, damping and vibration.
